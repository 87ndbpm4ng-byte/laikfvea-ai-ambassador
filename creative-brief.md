# AI Ambassador Creative Brief

## Document Status

This document is the master product design reference for the AI Ambassador project.

It aligns product vision, visitor experience, screen intent, visual direction, conversational behavior, exhibition operation, accessibility, and long-term development. Future product decisions should be evaluated against this brief, `AGENTS.md`, and `design.md`.

Instruction priority:

1. Current user direction.
2. `AGENTS.md`.
3. This creative brief.
4. `design.md`.

Where this brief and `design.md` differ, the stricter rule applies. In particular, the idle screen must never display the Laikfvea name, logo, wordmark, symbol, or identifying product branding.

This is a product design document. It does not authorize application development, UI construction, or product-claim creation.

---

## 1. Executive Summary

AI Ambassador is an interactive AI Product Specialist designed for exhibition kiosks. It helps a visitor move from curiosity to informed product understanding through a calm, guided, and responsive conversation.

The experience should feel like a thoughtful specialist is present at exactly the right moment. It should not feel like a generic chatbot, a digital catalogue, a sales script, or a demonstration of artificial intelligence for its own sake.

The product must perform in a demanding environment:

- Visitors may have only a few minutes.
- The surrounding exhibition may be noisy and visually crowded.
- The kiosk is shared by many people.
- Attention may be interrupted.
- Network quality may vary.
- Product and wellness information must remain accurate.
- The interface must be immediately understandable without instruction.

The product response is deliberate restraint. Every screen has one clear purpose. Every action is large, visible, and reversible. Every answer is grounded in approved information. Every transition communicates state. The result should feel closer to a premium product consultation than a software interface.

## 2. Project Vision

### 2.1 Vision statement

Create the most elegant and trustworthy way to understand a premium product at an exhibition, combining the clarity of a specialist conversation with the immediacy of a touch-first kiosk.

### 2.2 Product promise

Within a short interaction, a visitor should be able to:

- Understand what the relevant products are.
- Discover which product may fit their stated interest.
- Learn the meaningful differences between products.
- Ask a specific question in natural language.
- See only approved product facts and claims.
- Recognize uncertainty when information is unavailable.
- Continue with a human specialist when appropriate.
- Leave the kiosk confident about the next step.

### 2.3 Experience ambition

The experience should feel:

- As considered as premium hardware.
- As clear as a well-trained product specialist.
- As calm as high-quality wellness technology.
- As intuitive as a native iPad experience.
- As honest as an evidence-led product conversation.

### 2.4 What makes the product distinctive

The distinction is not feature quantity. It is the quality of the encounter.

AI Ambassador differentiates through:

- Fast orientation without visible complexity.
- Guided discovery that adapts to visitor intent.
- Product presentation that feels editorial rather than transactional.
- Answers grounded only in approved source material.
- A premium interface that remains useful in a public setting.
- Strong session privacy and predictable reset behavior.
- Graceful escalation to a human specialist.

### 2.5 Product principles

#### Product first, AI second

AI is the enabling capability, not the visual theme. Product understanding is always the central subject.

#### One meaningful decision at a time

Do not ask visitors to scan dense menus or understand a complicated flow. Each screen should move one decision forward.

#### Truth before persuasion

Never invent product specifications, scientific evidence, health benefits, availability, pricing, certifications, or performance claims. If the approved source does not contain an answer, say so plainly.

#### Premium through reduction

Choose fewer elements, clearer language, better imagery, larger spacing, and better response behavior over feature density.

#### Public by context, personal by experience

The kiosk is shared, but the interaction should feel attentive to the current visitor. Personalization must never compromise privacy.

#### Human help is part of the product

The assistant should know when a staff handoff is more useful than another generated answer.

## 3. Audience and Context

### 3.1 Primary visitors

The primary audience includes exhibition attendees who are:

- Encountering the product or company for the first time.
- Looking for a concise product explanation.
- Comparing products or use cases.
- Interested in a specific feature.
- Seeking reassurance before speaking with staff.
- Exploring independently while staff are occupied.

No assumption should be made about technical knowledge, product familiarity, age, language fluency, or accessibility needs.

### 3.2 Secondary users

Secondary users include:

- Exhibition staff who use the kiosk to support a live conversation.
- Brand and product teams who maintain approved content.
- Event operators responsible for uptime and reset behavior.
- Accessibility reviewers and compliance stakeholders.
- Future content editors and localization teams.

### 3.3 Exhibition conditions

Design for:

- High ambient noise.
- Strong or inconsistent venue lighting.
- Visitors standing at varying distances.
- Glare and fingerprints on the display.
- Short, interrupted sessions.
- Multiple people observing one interaction.
- Uncertain connectivity.
- Repeated daily use.
- A device that may operate unattended.

The product should never depend on quiet audio, hover behavior, fine motor precision, prior instruction, or uninterrupted attention.

### 3.4 Primary device

The primary design target is an iPad kiosk in portrait or landscape orientation.

The experience must:

- Be fully touch-first.
- Use generous targets and spacing.
- Keep important actions within comfortable reach.
- Avoid requiring external keyboards or mice.
- Preserve state across orientation changes.
- Recover cleanly from interruption.
- Work in full-screen kiosk mode without browser navigation.

## 4. End-to-End Visitor Journey

The ideal journey is brief, confident, and adaptive. It supports both a visitor who wants a recommendation and a visitor who already has a precise question.

### Phase 1: Notice

The visitor encounters a quiet idle screen.

The screen is brand-neutral. It does not show the Laikfvea name, logo, wordmark, symbol, product name, or product imagery. It communicates only that a touch interaction is available.

Visitor need:

- Understand that the screen is interactive.
- Feel comfortable initiating contact.

Experience goal:

- Invite without competing with the exhibition environment.

### Phase 2: Begin

The visitor deliberately touches the idle screen or its start control.

This action creates a new session. Brand identity may appear only after this explicit start.

Visitor need:

- Understand what the experience offers.
- See a clear first choice.

Experience goal:

- Establish trust and orient the visitor in one screen.

### Phase 3: Choose a path

The visitor chooses between a small number of meaningful paths, such as:

- Explore products.
- Find a suitable option.
- Compare products.
- Ask a question.

These are directional examples, not final labels. Labels must be validated against the approved product structure and kept short.

Visitor need:

- Enter through the path that matches current intent.

Experience goal:

- Avoid forcing every visitor through the same questionnaire.

### Phase 4: Express an interest

The visitor selects a guided topic or enters a natural-language question.

Visitor need:

- Explain what they care about without learning product terminology.

Experience goal:

- Collect only the context required to provide a useful next response.

### Phase 5: Receive guidance

The assistant provides a concise, grounded answer or recommendation.

The response leads with the answer, shows relevant product context, and distinguishes approved fact from interpretation. If information is missing, the assistant states that limitation.

Visitor need:

- Understand the recommendation and why it is relevant.

Experience goal:

- Deliver clarity without overselling or overwhelming.

### Phase 6: Explore evidence

The visitor may inspect approved product facts, compare options, review a product image, or ask a follow-up question.

Visitor need:

- Verify important details and understand meaningful differences.

Experience goal:

- Provide depth on demand without making the default experience dense.

### Phase 7: Decide the next step

The visitor chooses a next action:

- Ask another question.
- Compare a relevant alternative.
- Speak with a human specialist.
- End and reset the session.

Visitor need:

- Continue naturally without returning to the beginning.

Experience goal:

- Make the transition from information to action feel helpful, not sales-driven.

### Phase 8: Complete

The experience acknowledges completion and offers a clear final action.

Visitor need:

- Know that the interaction is complete.
- Understand what can happen next.

Experience goal:

- End with confidence and no ambiguity.

### Phase 9: Reset

The session clears all visitor-entered and generated personal context. The display returns to the brand-neutral idle screen.

Visitor need:

- Trust that personal interaction history is not visible to the next visitor.

Experience goal:

- Reset completely, quickly, and without flashing previous-session content.

## 5. Screen Framework

The experience should use the fewest screens necessary. Each screen has one primary purpose and one dominant action.

### 5.1 Idle screen

Purpose:

- Signal that the kiosk is available for interaction.
- Invite a deliberate touch to begin.
- Protect brand presentation and visitor privacy between sessions.

Required:

- Brand-neutral visual language.
- One concise invitation.
- One obvious touch target or whole-screen start behavior.
- No previous-session content.
- No audio autoplay.
- Minimal or no motion.

Prohibited:

- Laikfvea name.
- Laikfvea logo, wordmark, or symbol.
- Product names.
- Product imagery.
- Product claims.
- Customer data.
- Previous questions or answers.

Success condition:

- A first-time visitor understands that touching the screen will begin an experience.

### 5.2 Welcome and orientation screen

Purpose:

- Introduce the branded experience after the visitor starts.
- Explain the value of the Product Specialist.
- Present the first clear action.

Required:

- Approved brand identity.
- One concise welcome statement.
- One short explanation.
- One primary action.
- Optional language selection.
- Approved product or lifestyle imagery only if it strengthens orientation.

Success condition:

- The visitor knows what the assistant can help with and how to proceed.

### 5.3 Path selection screen

Purpose:

- Let visitors choose a path that matches their intent.

Required:

- Three to five large choices at most.
- Plain-language labels.
- One sentence of context only if necessary.
- Immediate pressed and selected feedback.

Success condition:

- The visitor can choose without reading product documentation.

### 5.4 Guided discovery screen

Purpose:

- Gather the minimum context needed for relevant guidance.

Required:

- One question at a time.
- A small number of meaningful choices.
- A visible way to go back or start over.
- Clear progress only when the flow has a real fixed sequence.

Prohibited:

- Long forms.
- Unnecessary personal questions.
- Medical screening.
- Questions that imply unapproved outcomes.

Success condition:

- The assistant has enough approved context to provide a relevant next response.

### 5.5 Open question screen

Purpose:

- Let a visitor ask a specific question in their own words.

Required:

- Large, clearly labeled input.
- Suggested questions relevant to the current context.
- Visible submit action.
- Clear listening state if voice input is available.
- A text and touch alternative to voice.

Success condition:

- The visitor can ask a question without understanding system syntax.

### 5.6 Thinking or loading state

Purpose:

- Confirm that the request was received.
- Explain that the system is preparing a response.

Required:

- Stable layout.
- Specific status language.
- Calm visual feedback.
- Recovery guidance if waiting becomes unusually long.

Prohibited:

- Artificial AI spectacle.
- Fake progress percentages.
- Indefinite animation without recovery.
- Typewriter output.

Success condition:

- The visitor understands that the kiosk is working and has not frozen.

### 5.7 Answer screen

Purpose:

- Deliver a clear, trustworthy answer.

Required:

- Direct answer first.
- Approved product evidence or source reference where relevant.
- Clear limitation when information cannot be confirmed.
- One useful next action.
- Optional follow-up paths.

Prohibited:

- Dense chat bubbles.
- Internal reasoning.
- Invented product facts.
- Invented scientific or health claims.
- Unsupported certainty.

Success condition:

- The visitor can summarize the answer after a brief read.

### 5.8 Product recommendation screen

Purpose:

- Present a relevant product based on the visitor's stated need.

Required:

- Approved product name and imagery.
- One clear explanation of relevance.
- Verified facts only.
- Important limitations or conditions.
- A next step such as compare, ask, or speak with staff.

Success condition:

- The visitor understands why the product is relevant without feeling pressured.

### 5.9 Product detail screen

Purpose:

- Provide focused depth about one product.

Required:

- Strong approved image.
- Product name.
- Short positioning statement.
- Grouped approved specifications or evidence.
- Clear return and next actions.

Prohibited:

- Long undifferentiated specification tables.
- Fabricated numbers.
- Decorative technical diagrams without informational value.
- Unapproved medical or wellness interpretation.

Success condition:

- The visitor can identify the product's relevant qualities and limitations.

### 5.10 Comparison screen

Purpose:

- Explain meaningful differences between two relevant products.

Required:

- Two products at a time on iPad.
- Consistent comparison topics.
- Verified values.
- Plain summary of who each option may suit.
- No visual declaration of a winner unless approved product logic supports it.

Success condition:

- The visitor can distinguish the options without reading every detail.

### 5.11 Human handoff screen

Purpose:

- Connect the visitor with exhibition staff when human support is more useful.

Required:

- Clear reason for the handoff.
- One primary action.
- Confirmation of what happens next.
- Privacy-conscious handling of any contact details.

Success condition:

- The visitor knows how and when human help will arrive.

### 5.12 Completion screen

Purpose:

- Close the active journey cleanly.

Required:

- Short acknowledgment.
- Clear next step.
- Visible session-ending or restart action.

Success condition:

- The visitor understands that the session is complete.

### 5.13 Inactivity warning

Purpose:

- Protect privacy while avoiding an unexpected reset.

Required:

- Clear countdown.
- Large “Continue session” action.
- Secondary option to end.
- At least the approved accessible extension period.
- No interruption while the visitor is actively typing, speaking, or interacting.

Success condition:

- An active visitor can preserve the session, while an abandoned session resets safely.

### 5.14 Offline or service interruption screen

Purpose:

- Preserve usefulness when connectivity or AI service is unavailable.

Required:

- Plain explanation.
- Distinction between locally available product content and unavailable AI features.
- Retry action.
- Human help option where operationally possible.

Success condition:

- The visitor can continue product discovery or recover without losing orientation.

### 5.15 Maintenance screen

Purpose:

- Prevent a broken experience from reaching visitors.

Required:

- Calm, minimal message.
- No raw errors.
- Protected staff recovery information.

Success condition:

- Visitors see a controlled unavailable state and staff can diagnose safely.

## 6. Visual Experience

### 6.1 Desired impression

The visual system should feel quiet, precise, bright, and materially considered.

The experience draws from:

- Apple's focus and interaction clarity.
- Scandinavian reduction and functional warmth.
- Premium wellness technology without clinical coldness.
- Laikfvea's approved visual identity after the session begins.

The visual system must never imitate Apple assets or become a generic luxury template.

### 6.2 Core visual language

- White is the dominant background.
- Large whitespace establishes calm and hierarchy.
- Montserrat is the sole interface type family.
- Black is the primary action color.
- `#7EAFC3` is the restrained brand accent.
- The accessible strong blue defined in `design.md` is used for accent text and focus.
- Borders and spacing carry most grouping.
- Shadows are rare and subtle.
- Product imagery is clean, approved, and given room to breathe.

### 6.3 Composition

- One focal point per screen.
- One dominant action per decision area.
- No more than two major columns on iPad.
- Left alignment for reading and comparison.
- Center alignment only for idle, welcome, single-prompt, and completion moments when it improves focus.
- Main actions remain visible without exploratory scrolling.
- Empty space is not filled for decoration.

### 6.4 Material treatment

Surfaces should feel solid and quiet.

Use:

- White and very light neutral surfaces.
- Fine borders.
- Consistent soft radii.
- A single subtle floating shadow only for necessary overlays.

Do not use:

- Gradients.
- Glassmorphism.
- Backdrop blur.
- Heavy shadows.
- Outer glow.
- Neon.
- Decorative translucency.
- Excessive cards or pills.

### 6.5 Typography

Typography carries most of the premium character.

- Use Montserrat at approved weights only.
- Keep headlines short and confident.
- Use large body text suitable for arm's-length reading.
- Maintain generous line height.
- Keep paragraphs narrow and brief.
- Use sentence case.
- Avoid decorative italics, excessive bold, all-caps instructions, or novelty type.

### 6.6 Color behavior

- Blue is an accent, not a second primary action color.
- Black actions carry decision weight.
- Semantic success, warning, and error colors retain their meanings.
- Never rely on color alone.
- Avoid multiple accent families.
- Maintain strong contrast under bright exhibition lighting.

### 6.7 Visual hierarchy

Every screen should answer, in order:

1. Where am I?
2. What is this asking or telling me?
3. What should I do next?
4. What can I do if this is not what I need?

If a visitor cannot answer these quickly, the screen is too complex.

## 7. Motion Principles

### 7.1 Role of motion

Motion exists to:

- Acknowledge touch.
- Confirm a change in state.
- Guide attention between related content.
- Communicate that the system is working.
- Preserve spatial understanding across transitions.

Motion does not exist to make the product look futuristic or to fill visual silence.

### 7.2 Motion character

Motion should be:

- Soft.
- Short.
- Predictable.
- Physically credible.
- Easy to ignore after it has communicated its purpose.

### 7.3 Approved motion

- Immediate pressed feedback on touch.
- Short fade and small positional shift for content changes.
- Subtle selection feedback on choice tiles.
- Calm panel and dialog transitions.
- Grouped answer reveal by meaningful block.
- Stable loading feedback.

### 7.4 Prohibited motion

- Typewriter answers.
- Text scrambling.
- Parallax.
- Scroll hijacking.
- Large zoom transitions.
- Elastic bounce.
- Magnetic controls.
- Autoplay carousels.
- Continuous floating or breathing elements.
- Confetti.
- Particles.
- Animated decorative backgrounds.

### 7.5 Reduced motion

The experience must remain complete with motion removed.

Under reduced-motion preference:

- Remove movement and scale.
- Use instant state changes or very short opacity transitions.
- Keep progress and loading meaning visible.
- Never make information dependent on animation.

## 8. Product Presentation

### 8.1 Product presentation goal

Products should feel tangible, understandable, and credible. The experience should reveal the product through relevant context, not through dense selling language.

### 8.2 Approved information only

All product content must come from an approved source.

Never invent:

- Dimensions.
- Materials.
- Capacity.
- Compatibility.
- Performance.
- Pricing.
- Availability.
- Warranty.
- Certifications.
- Test results.
- Scientific evidence.
- Health or wellness outcomes.

If information is not available, the interface should omit it or state that it cannot be confirmed.

### 8.3 Product imagery

- Use approved high-resolution photography or renders.
- Show products at a useful scale.
- Prefer clean white or light neutral surroundings.
- Maintain consistent lighting and color temperature.
- Show authentic use context only when approved.
- Preserve true proportions and materials.
- Avoid labels over the image.
- Do not use a product image on the idle screen.

### 8.4 Product copy

Product copy should:

- Lead with a clear, approved differentiator.
- Explain the relevance to the visitor's stated interest.
- Separate facts from interpretation.
- State conditions and limitations.
- Avoid hype.
- Avoid pseudo-scientific language.
- Avoid guaranteed outcomes.

### 8.5 Recommendations

A recommendation is guidance, not a verdict.

Every recommendation should include:

- The product.
- Why it may fit the stated need.
- The approved evidence or fact supporting that relevance.
- Any important limitation.
- A way to compare or ask a follow-up question.

### 8.6 Comparisons

Comparisons should focus on meaningful differences.

- Compare no more than two products at once on iPad.
- Group facts by visitor-relevant topic.
- Keep units and qualifiers exact.
- Avoid scoring products unless an approved methodology exists.
- Do not visually declare one product superior without approved grounds.
- Summarize the type of visitor need each product may address.

### 8.7 Health and scientific content

The assistant must never:

- Diagnose.
- Treat.
- Prescribe.
- Promise prevention or cure.
- Turn product information into personalized medical advice.
- Strengthen an approved claim.
- Create scientific explanations without an approved source.

When a visitor requests medical guidance, the assistant should state its limitation and guide the visitor toward an appropriate human or professional source.

## 9. Conversation Philosophy

### 9.1 Role

The assistant behaves like a calm, informed product specialist.

It is:

- Direct.
- Respectful.
- Concise.
- Curious when clarification is genuinely needed.
- Honest about uncertainty.
- Grounded in approved content.

It is not:

- Overfamiliar.
- Performative.
- Pushy.
- Medical.
- Speculative.
- Verbose by default.

### 9.2 Answer structure

A strong answer follows this order:

1. Direct response.
2. Brief explanation.
3. Relevant approved product fact or evidence.
4. Limitation or condition if needed.
5. One useful next action.

### 9.3 Clarification

Ask a clarification question only when the answer would materially change.

- Ask one question at a time.
- Prefer choices when they reduce effort.
- Do not ask for personal data unless necessary and approved.
- Do not make visitors repeat information already given in the session.

### 9.4 Tone

Use plain, professional language.

- Keep sentences short.
- Explain technical terms.
- Avoid jargon, hype, filler, and false enthusiasm.
- Avoid emojis and decorative punctuation.
- Do not mimic human emotion or imply consciousness.
- Never claim certainty beyond the approved content.

### 9.5 Conversation memory

Within an active session:

- Remember relevant visitor intent.
- Avoid asking the same question twice.
- Use prior answers only to improve the current interaction.

At session end:

- Clear conversational context.
- Prevent any content from appearing to the next visitor.
- Do not expose previous prompts in idle or attract mode.

### 9.6 Source behavior

The assistant should make approved source grounding visible when it improves trust.

- Attach a source or product reference near consequential claims.
- Do not invent a citation.
- Do not imply independent verification when none exists.
- Clearly distinguish missing information from negative information.

### 9.7 Refusal and uncertainty

When the assistant cannot answer:

- Say what cannot be confirmed.
- Explain why in plain language when useful.
- Offer an approved related topic.
- Offer a human specialist when appropriate.

The refusal should remain helpful without filling the gap with speculation.

## 10. Exhibition Behaviour

### 10.1 Idle behavior

- Remain visually quiet.
- Show no Laikfvea name, logo, wordmark, symbol, product name, or product imagery.
- Show no previous-session content.
- Play no audio.
- Use minimal or no automatic motion.
- Begin only after deliberate visitor interaction.

### 10.2 Active behavior

- Keep the current purpose visible.
- Keep “Start over” available.
- Avoid browser chrome and external navigation.
- Prevent accidental duplicate actions.
- Preserve context during normal orientation changes.
- Never expose technical system details.

### 10.3 Inactivity

- Detect inactivity without interrupting active typing, voice, or touch.
- Warn before resetting.
- Provide a large action to continue.
- Announce the warning accessibly.
- Reset only after the approved period.

### 10.4 Reset

Reset must:

- Clear visitor input.
- Clear generated session context.
- Clear navigation and scroll state.
- Stop active audio or listening.
- Remove branded and product content before idle appears.
- Avoid flashing the previous screen.
- Return to a brand-neutral idle state.

### 10.5 Network resilience

The kiosk should degrade in layers:

1. Full guided and AI experience.
2. Locally available product discovery without AI.
3. Controlled unavailable state with retry or staff guidance.

Network failure should never produce a blank screen, raw error, endless loader, or lost orientation.

### 10.6 Staff operation

Staff-facing controls should:

- Remain hidden from normal visitors.
- Require a protected action.
- Expose only the operational detail necessary.
- Never reveal credentials, prompts, private data, or raw logs publicly.

### 10.7 Environmental behavior

- Do not depend on audio.
- Avoid low-contrast content that disappears under glare.
- Keep text readable from standing distance.
- Keep key actions reachable in both orientations.
- Avoid high-energy animation that competes with surrounding exhibits.
- Allow a staff member and visitor to view the screen together.

## 11. Accessibility

### 11.1 Standard

WCAG 2.2 AA is the minimum acceptance standard.

The product should be designed for visitors with varied:

- Vision.
- Hearing.
- Motor ability.
- Cognitive load tolerance.
- Language fluency.
- Technology familiarity.

### 11.2 Touch accessibility

- Use targets of at least 56 by 56 CSS pixels where practical.
- Keep at least 12px between distinct targets.
- Do not require precise gestures.
- Do not place destructive actions beside primary actions.
- Provide visible pressed feedback.

### 11.3 Visual accessibility

- Maintain required contrast in all states.
- Use the accessible strong blue for accent text on white.
- Do not rely on color alone.
- Keep body text large enough for arm's-length reading.
- Avoid dense paragraphs.
- Preserve legibility under glare.

### 11.4 Keyboard and focus

Even a touch-first kiosk must:

- Support logical keyboard navigation.
- Show visible focus.
- Use semantic control roles.
- Trap and restore focus correctly in dialogs.
- Avoid focus loss during async content updates.

### 11.5 Screen-reader behavior

- Use semantic headings and landmarks.
- Give every control an accessible name.
- Associate fields, hints, and errors.
- Announce loading and completed responses without repeated interruption.
- Keep dynamic updates concise.

### 11.6 Motion

- Respect reduced-motion preferences.
- Avoid vestibular triggers.
- Do not communicate meaning only through movement.

### 11.7 Time limits

- Warn before timeout.
- Allow the visitor to extend the session.
- Provide adequate response time.
- Do not reset during active interaction.
- Ensure countdown behavior does not create unnecessary urgency.

### 11.8 Audio and voice

If voice is introduced:

- Require explicit activation.
- Show listening and stopped states.
- Provide an equivalent text and touch route.
- Provide captions or text equivalents.
- Include visible stop and volume controls.

### 11.9 Language and comprehension

- Use plain language.
- Keep instructions short.
- Explain technical terms.
- Support localization and text expansion.
- Avoid fixed layouts that break with longer translations.

### 11.10 Accessibility acceptance

No screen is complete until accessibility has been checked in:

- Default.
- Focus.
- Selected.
- Disabled.
- Loading.
- Error.
- Offline.
- Timeout.
- Reduced-motion states.

## 12. Content Governance

### 12.1 Source of truth

Customer-facing product content must be drawn from an approved, versioned source.

Each approved fact should be traceable to:

- Product documentation.
- Approved marketing copy.
- Verified technical data.
- Approved scientific or regulatory material.
- Explicit product-owner approval.

### 12.2 Missing content

When content is missing:

- Do not invent it.
- Do not infer it from similar products.
- Do not use sample values in production.
- Mark the content gap for the project team.
- Use a safe fallback response for visitors.

### 12.3 Review responsibilities

Before release, appropriate owners should approve:

- Product names.
- Product specifications.
- Comparative statements.
- Health and wellness language.
- Scientific references.
- Disclaimers.
- Pricing and availability.
- Localization.

### 12.4 Change control

Changes to product facts or claims should be reviewed independently of interface changes. A visual redesign must not silently rewrite approved meaning.

## 13. Success Criteria

### 13.1 Visitor success

A successful visitor:

- Starts without instruction.
- Understands the assistant's purpose.
- Reaches a useful answer quickly.
- Can identify relevant product differences.
- Recognizes when information is unavailable.
- Knows how to continue with a human specialist.
- Leaves no personal session content behind.

### 13.2 Experience quality

The product succeeds when:

- The interface feels calm in a busy venue.
- The main action is obvious on every screen.
- The experience remains premium in portrait and landscape.
- Motion is subtle and informative.
- Product presentation feels credible.
- Accessibility is integral rather than supplemental.
- Network issues degrade gracefully.

### 13.3 Trust quality

Trust is protected when:

- No product specification is invented.
- No health or scientific claim is invented or strengthened.
- Uncertainty is visible.
- Sources are not fabricated.
- Session data is cleared.
- Human escalation is available.

### 13.4 Operational quality

The kiosk should:

- Recover from service interruptions.
- Reset reliably.
- Avoid previous-session leakage.
- Remain usable under exhibition conditions.
- Provide controlled maintenance behavior.

Final quantitative targets should be defined only after a measurable pilot plan is approved. Do not invent conversion, dwell-time, satisfaction, or accuracy targets.

## 14. Non-Goals

The initial product is not:

- A complete ecommerce store.
- A medical or diagnostic assistant.
- A replacement for exhibition staff.
- A general-purpose chatbot.
- A product-specification authoring system.
- A customer account portal.
- A social experience.
- A visual demonstration of AI effects.
- A dense catalogue of every available fact.

Features outside the core visitor journey should be justified by a clear exhibition need.

## 15. Future Roadmap

The roadmap should progress from trustworthy core experience to broader capability. Each phase requires product, content, accessibility, operational, and privacy review.

### Phase 0: Foundation

Purpose:

- Establish the approved product content model.
- Confirm exhibition hardware and kiosk constraints.
- Validate session privacy and reset rules.
- Finalize the design system and reusable screen architecture.
- Define human handoff operations.

Exit criteria:

- Approved content sources exist.
- Product and claim owners are identified.
- Idle, active, timeout, reset, offline, and maintenance behavior are defined.

### Phase 1: Guided product specialist

Purpose:

- Deliver the core guided journey.
- Support product exploration, grounded questions, recommendation context, and human handoff.

Priorities:

- Brand-neutral idle state.
- Branded welcome after deliberate start.
- Guided intent selection.
- Grounded answer presentation.
- Product detail and comparison.
- Session reset.
- Accessibility.
- Offline fallback.

### Phase 2: Exhibition pilot

Purpose:

- Validate the experience in a real venue.

Research areas:

- Whether visitors understand the idle invitation.
- Time to first useful answer.
- Where visitors abandon or request staff.
- Portrait and landscape ergonomics.
- Glare, noise, and standing distance.
- Timeout and reset behavior.
- Content gaps discovered through real questions.

Pilot analytics must avoid collecting unnecessary personal or conversational data.

### Phase 3: Content operations

Purpose:

- Make approved information easier to maintain and audit.

Potential capabilities:

- Versioned product content.
- Claim approval workflow.
- Source traceability.
- Localization workflow.
- Content freshness alerts.
- Event-specific product availability.

No editing system should allow unapproved claims to publish directly.

### Phase 4: Multilingual experience

Purpose:

- Support additional exhibition audiences.

Requirements:

- Professional translation of product and health language.
- Layout testing with expanded text.
- Language-specific voice and tone review.
- Accessible language switching.
- Per-language source approval.

### Phase 5: Voice interaction

Purpose:

- Offer an additional input mode where exhibition conditions support it.

Requirements:

- Explicit activation.
- Clear listening state.
- Text and touch equivalent.
- Noise-resilient behavior.
- Privacy review.
- Visible stop control.
- No audio autoplay.

Voice should not proceed if venue noise makes it less reliable than touch.

### Phase 6: Staff-assisted mode

Purpose:

- Let exhibition staff use the specialist as a shared consultation tool.

Potential capabilities:

- Staff-selected presentation paths.
- Approved deep-dive content.
- Simple handoff context.
- Protected operational controls.

The public experience must remain simple and independent.

### Phase 7: Learning and refinement

Purpose:

- Improve relevance from aggregated, privacy-conscious evidence.

Potential inputs:

- Unanswered question categories.
- Most-used guided paths.
- Common comparison topics.
- Operational failures.
- Staff feedback.
- Accessibility findings.

The system must not learn new product facts or health claims directly from visitor conversations.

### Phase 8: Broader deployment

Purpose:

- Adapt the proven system to additional exhibitions, showrooms, or approved retail environments.

Requirements:

- Configurable event content.
- Hardware profiles.
- Localization.
- Remote operational monitoring.
- Strict brand and content governance.
- Consistent accessibility acceptance.

Expansion should preserve the same premium simplicity. Deployment scale must not create a feature-heavy interface.

## 16. Decision Framework

When evaluating a new feature or design direction, ask:

1. Does it help a visitor understand a product?
2. Does it improve trust, clarity, accessibility, or exhibition resilience?
3. Is the required content approved and traceable?
4. Can the experience remain simple on iPad?
5. Does it protect public-session privacy?
6. Can it fail gracefully?
7. Is a human specialist a better solution?
8. Can the same outcome be achieved with fewer elements?

If two approaches are equally valid, choose the more premium and minimal solution.

Do not approve a feature merely because the technology can support it.

## 17. Experience Acceptance Checklist

### Vision

- [ ] The screen or feature supports product understanding.
- [ ] AI remains secondary to the product experience.
- [ ] The result prioritizes quality over feature quantity.

### Journey

- [ ] The current purpose is clear.
- [ ] One dominant next action is visible.
- [ ] The visitor can recover, go back, or start over.
- [ ] Session completion and reset are clear.

### Idle and privacy

- [ ] Idle mode contains no Laikfvea name, logo, wordmark, symbol, product name, or product imagery.
- [ ] No previous-session content is visible.
- [ ] The session begins only after deliberate interaction.
- [ ] Reset removes all visitor context before idle appears.

### Product truth

- [ ] Every specification comes from an approved source.
- [ ] Every scientific or health statement is approved.
- [ ] Missing information is not inferred.
- [ ] Comparisons preserve exact units and qualifiers.
- [ ] Uncertainty is visible.

### Visual quality

- [ ] The experience follows `design.md`.
- [ ] White space, typography, alignment, and imagery establish hierarchy.
- [ ] Primary actions are black.
- [ ] Blue remains a restrained accent.
- [ ] No gradient, glass effect, glow, or heavy shadow appears.
- [ ] The layout works in iPad portrait and landscape.

### Motion

- [ ] Every animation communicates feedback, progress, or state.
- [ ] Motion is short and subtle.
- [ ] Reduced-motion behavior is complete.
- [ ] No decorative continuous animation is present.

### Conversation

- [ ] The answer leads with useful information.
- [ ] Tone is concise, calm, and professional.
- [ ] Follow-up questions are necessary and singular.
- [ ] Sources are real and traceable.
- [ ] Human escalation is available where needed.

### Exhibition behavior

- [ ] The experience does not rely on audio or hover.
- [ ] Offline behavior is controlled.
- [ ] Timeout behavior is accessible.
- [ ] Maintenance behavior hides technical details.
- [ ] Staff-only controls are protected.

### Accessibility

- [ ] WCAG 2.2 AA is met.
- [ ] Touch targets meet kiosk standards.
- [ ] Contrast, focus, semantics, and dynamic announcements are correct.
- [ ] Language is plain.
- [ ] Voice, if present, has touch and text equivalents.

## 18. Final Creative Standard

AI Ambassador should make advanced technology feel invisible and product understanding feel immediate.

The visitor should remember:

- The clarity of the answer.
- The quality of the product presentation.
- The calmness of the interaction.
- The confidence to take a next step.

The visitor should not remember:

- A complicated interface.
- A flashy AI demonstration.
- A long questionnaire.
- An unsupported claim.
- A previous visitor's session.

The best version of this product is not the one with the most capabilities. It is the one that creates the clearest, most trustworthy product conversation in the shortest, most elegant way.
